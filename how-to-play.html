How to Play

Play Municipality Tycoon

You are the mayor of Clifton. Every other week the city council meets. Developers want to build, residents want to be heard, and every decision you make shows up in your meters. Survive your term.

Your goal

The object of the game: keep your seat as mayor, without being voted out or removed, and grow Clifton's population past a target by the end of your term. The target is 12,500 residents in the 12-week term and 16,500 in the 52-week term (the city starts at 12,000). The current goal shows in the City Growth panel. Finish above it and you are re-elected with a win. Finish below it and you survive, but it is not a win.

Finish your term as mayor with the city in good shape. You can play a 12-week quick term (about an hour) or a 52-week full term (several hours). There is no single way to win. A good term balances jobs and taxes against the environment, and keeps the public, the council and the developers from turning on you.

You lose if public trust collapses and voters recall you, the environment fails and the state steps in, or a corruption scandal puts you in court.

Meeting weeks and off weeks

The calendar alternates. Odd weeks are council meetings. Even weeks are off weeks.

On a meeting week

  • You read routine city business and hear from the council.
  • Planning reports on projects that are already building.
  • The agenda: a budget hearing every six weeks, smaller items (petitions, variances, small projects, and more), and one main project with a vote.

On an off week

  • Take a private meeting with a developer or lobbyist. This is where bribes and favors show up.
  • Choose two activities: inspect a site, hold office hours, walk the neighborhood, give a press interview, hold a work session, take a council member to lunch, or campaign if a recall is coming.
  • Projects keep moving and incidents can still happen. Urgent ones (blackouts, spills) call an emergency session. The rest wait for the next meeting.

At the meeting: the main project

Each meeting has one main project, from a small shop to a billion-dollar build. The developer pitches, Zoning and the Fire Marshal give staff comments, the City Attorney gives advice, the council discusses, and residents speak at the podium.

Add conditions

Then you propose conditions. The list is different for every project. A data center might offer a groundwater pumping cap, or a dedicated substation. A quarry might offer dust suppression or a blasting schedule.

  • Good conditions protect the city or win over council members, but cost the developer. Push too far and they walk away.
  • Developer-friendly conditions ease their burden, but come with real downsides like more incidents or lost tax revenue.
  • Uncertain conditions might pay off and might backfire. You find out after approval.

Your motion

You can recommend approval, recommend denial, or table the item for a week. When you table, members ask for studies (hydrology, noise, traffic, fiscal and more). The reports come back next week and can change the numbers, the debate and what residents say. The council then votes: three of five members must say yes.

The council is not a rubber stamp. Dr. Pondwater cares about the environment, Cashmore about business, Hammerstein about jobs, Porchlight about neighborhoods, and Ledgerwood about the budget. Know who you are asking.

The City Attorney

The City Attorney gives advice that is sometimes sound and sometimes bad, and you are not told which. You can follow it, ignore it, or pay for a second opinion. The consequences show up later.

After approval

Projects move through Permitting, Site Prep, Construction, Commissioning and Operating, one stage at a time. As they do, things go wrong or right: unpermitted wells, contaminated or wasted water, noise, blackouts, water main breaks, but also new roads, parks and donations.

When something goes wrong you choose a response. Many incidents have their own options beyond a fine or a stop-work order. A strong response protects the city but costs you goodwill with the developer. Letting a developer off the hook brings angry residents to the podium.

Developers also remember. A company with a record of problems pitches differently from one with a record of good deeds, and some developers are simply slipperier than others.

Backgrounds, scenarios and endings

  • Background: on the title screen pick who you were before you became mayor: a business owner, union organizer, retired teacher or retired police sergeant. Each starts you with different friends and enemies on the council. Or let the game surprise you.
  • Scenario: a standard town, a sleepy town with extra cash, a boom town with strained utilities and a higher population goal, a town in debt, or a town recovering from a tornado.
  • Council traits: every game each council member gets a random trait (such as Penny-pincher, Tree hugger or Dealmaker), shown next to their name. It shifts what they care about.
  • Epilogue: when your term ends you get a final front page, a then-and-now table, a grade, any new achievements, and a result you can copy and share.
  • Accessibility: the Menu has a text size setting, a color-blind friendly palette and a reduce-motion option.

The city

  • Council relationships: each member has a standing with you. Votes that go their way build it. Take them to lunch, do favors, or ask for a promised vote on a big item. Members also have personal stories that unfold over your term.
  • Growth: jobs bring residents. Build enough homes or rents rise and trust falls. Too many trucks cause congestion.
  • Renewable projects: solar and wind farms take a little load off the power grid, more for larger projects. They also cover land and habitat, which hurts the environment, and they can cause habitat loss, runoff, and bird and bat deaths.
  • Camera networks: an automated license plate camera network can recover stolen cars and find missing people, and it earns a little trust each week. It can also cause wrongful arrests, officer misuse, data sharing and breaches. Conditions such as warrants, audit logs, short retention and no outside sharing cut the risk.
  • The Police Chief: he joins staff comments on camera and crime-prone projects and weighs in on crime-related incidents.
  • The Clifton Courier: if you or a council member says something dumb or demeaning, the Courier may run it on the front page before the next meeting. It only makes print if most of 20 polled residents react the same way. A front page costs trust, and the member who made the remark takes a hit with you.
  • Public Works: its budget level sets how clean and plowed your streets are. Boost keeps potholes filled, snow moving and festival messes cleaned up, and a cut lets everything slide. A street condition meter in City Systems tracks it. The crew lead and the Director give their view on resident requests, community walks, seasonal matters and budget hearings, and after every festival you decide how to handle cleanup.
  • The Chamber of Commerce: its CEO, Wally Handshaw, brings proposals tied to your development projects. Some are real community deals (workforce training, local suppliers, infrastructure money, a community benefits agreement). Others are quiet favors with hidden costs: a near-total tax abatement, a secret water deal, a below-market land sale, a gag order, a consulting fee for the mayor, or a skipped safety review. You can accept, accept with public safeguards, ask the City Attorney to review the fine print first, or decline. Secret deals can leak through an open records request, and then you decide how to respond. You can also have breakfast with Wally on an off week.
  • Orion Power: the utility's community relations manager answers residents' energy questions with everything except answers. You can press him for a straight answer or let it go. Orion also asks for rate increases, and a deal with them affects the grid.
  • Scandals: beyond bribes, the Courier can break a dozen kinds of scandal: a developer-paid conference trip, campaign donors, a leaked recording, a no-bid contract for a relative, a council group text that breaks open-meetings law, a councilmember's expense report, a worker who says he was demoted for speaking up, and more. You choose how to respond: come clean, deny it, blame staff, attack the story, defend or censure a member, or send it to the county ethics board. An ethics probe takes a few weeks, then the board clears you, reprimands you or finds a serious violation. Denying a scandal that is true makes it much worse, and a scandal is more likely when your scandal risk is high.
  • Seasons: the year runs spring, summer, autumn and winter, and each season has its own festival and its own matters to decide: flood watches, pool staffing, school traffic, snow plows. Heat, floods and blizzards hit in their own seasons, and a little preparation (sandbags, cooling centers, plow crews) can head off the next one.
  • Council stories: each member has up to four personal story moments over the term, and two members may feud and ask you to take a side.
  • More Courier stories: besides gaffes, the Courier may cover project openings, forgiven incidents, a bribe scandal, population milestones, festivals, council feuds, and letters from readers.
  • Ordinances: every so often the council can adopt a rule that applies to all future projects, such as water recycling or local hire. It can also repeal one.
  • Crises: heat waves, droughts, floods, a tornado, cyber attacks, strikes and scandals test you. Funding Public Works and Public Safety makes them cheaper to handle.
  • Budget: the property tax rate and four departments (Public Works, Public Safety, Parks and Library, Inspections) each have a real effect on trust, incidents and costs.
  • Seasons and priorities: festivals mark the seasons, and in the full term you pick a priority every quarter.
Bribes are tempting and they pay well. Accepted bribes can leak to the press weeks later. A leak costs trust, and denying it can make it worse.

Sewer and trash

Every project and every new resident leans on two city systems. Both show as bars in the City Systems panel, and both can cost you trust and the environment if you ignore them.

The sewer plant

  • What loads it: population, the workers each operating project hires, and each project's wastewater. The agenda panel shows a proposal's workers hired and sewer load before you vote.
  • Overflows: above about 85% the plant can overflow. That is an emergency incident at the biggest user. You can fine them, forgive them, or order a pretreatment system that cuts their load by 40%.
  • Over 100%: growth slows, the environment and trust drain, and residents start to leave over sewage backups.
  • Fixes: fund a sewer plant upgrade in an off-week work session ($180K, lowers the load for good). Funding Public Works helps a little.

The landfill

  • What fills it: population, hired workers, and projects under construction.
  • The proposal: when the landfill reaches about 70% full, a landfill expansion proposal comes to the agenda. Expand the existing landfill, approve a new regional site, haul trash away at $9K a week, fund recycling, or table it. Tabled items come back after a few weeks.
  • If it fills up: above 90% the environment and trust slowly slip, and past 100% residents leave over garbage piling up.
  • Other fixes: recycling permanently cuts trash by 20%. Landfill compaction in a work session adds a bit of capacity.
In the 12-week term the landfill often gets close to the limit without needing a proposal. In the full 52-week term, expect to deal with it several times.

Tips for a new mayor

  • Do not approve everything. Water use and incidents add up, and a good project with a bad developer is still a bad project.
  • Table a project you are unsure about. A study can change the numbers in your favor, or save you from a mistake.
  • Use the off weeks. A site inspection can catch a problem before it becomes an incident, and a council lunch can win a vote you would otherwise lose.
  • Fund inspections if you approve a lot of projects. It lowers incident odds across the board.
  • Watch the City Systems panel. High grid load causes blackouts, high water draw causes main breaks, and a full sewer plant or landfill hurts trust and the environment.
  • Keep a little cash. Crises arrive without warning, and they cost less with good public works and safety funding.
  • If trust is falling, get ahead of it: speak to protesters, fund a petition, or hold a listening session.

FAQ

How long does it take?

A 12-week quick term takes about an hour. The 52-week full term takes several hours, and you can save and come back any time.

Is there a way to speed it up?

Yes. Use the Speed button or press F. Max speed advances dialogue on its own.

Does the game need an internet connection?

Only to load it. Once the page is open it runs entirely in your browser.

Is my progress shared with anyone?

No. Your save lives in your own browser and never leaves it.

Where do I send ideas or bug reports?

Use the Feedback button in the game. It copies your notes along with your current week and meters.
